## How to read this file

Each section states a dispute, tabulates the competing studies, and ends with a
line labelled **What this paper can say about it**. Sections are ordered
roughly by how directly the paper's design bears on them. Three sections are
flagged **THREAT** rather than opportunity: they identify places where the
literature could be marshalled *against* the paper's argument.

---

## 1. The Legitimacy Puzzle (Booth & Seligson) — named, never solved at the macro level

### State of the dispute

Booth and Seligson named the puzzle in 2009: political scientists have long
worried that declining citizen support for the regime threatens democratic
survival, yet nobody has empirically linked that decline to survival or
breakdown. Their own eight-nation LAPOP test found that disaffected Latin
Americans did not withdraw from politics or turn to destabilizing protest; they
participated at high rates through conventional and alternative channels. The
puzzle "resolved" into a paradox — dissatisfied citizens can be a source of
reform rather than a threat — but the resolution was purchased at the level of
individual behavior in a single region at a single time point (2004 surveys,
eight countries). The macro-level version of the puzzle, whether measured
discontent predicts coded regime events, was left open, and reviewers noted the
book could not really test it: with eight countries the authors had to enter
macrosocial variables one at a time.

Seventeen years on, no study in the verified set has closed it. That is the
opening this paper occupies. But note the asymmetry the puzzle sets up: Booth
and Seligson looked hardest at *democracies* (and hybrid regimes) in one region,
and found nothing. This paper also finds nothing in democracies. The puzzle is
therefore not refuted by finding (a); it is *localized* — the destabilizing
effect lives in autocracies, precisely where Booth and Seligson's design could
not look.

| Study | Position | Evidence base | Level | Verdict on the link |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Booth & Seligson 2009 (Cambridge UP) | Low legitimacy does NOT destabilize | LAPOP surveys, 8 Latin American nations, 2004 | Individual behavior, 8 macro units | Null; names the puzzle |
| Lipset 1959 *APSR*; Easton 1975 | Legitimacy is a precondition of stability | Theory + development–democracy correlations | Macro, asserted | Asserted, never tested with measured legitimacy |
| Norris 2011 *Democratic Deficit* | Deficit fuels critical engagement, not breakdown | 50+ societies, comparative survey | Attitudinal, no event DV | Null / reinterpretation |
| van Ham, Thomassen, Aarts & Andeweg (eds.) 2017 (OUP) | The secular decline itself is "limited, questionable, or absent" | Trend analysis, established democracies | Aggregate trends | Denies the premise |
| **This paper** | Discontent destabilizes autocracies only | PPD latent estimates, 136 countries 1968–2023, ERT events | Country-year hazard | Positive in autocracies; null in democracies |

**What this paper can say about it:** it supplies the missing macro test and
splits the puzzle in two. Finding (a) says the legitimacy puzzle is a
*democracy-specific* puzzle: Booth and Seligson's null replicates and
generalizes across 136 countries, while the classical Lipset/Easton expectation
holds in autocracies. The paper should claim the resolution as regime-conditional
rather than claim to have overturned the null.

## 2. The Punishment Puzzle (Davenport) — repression's effects, still unresolved after 50 years

### State of the dispute

Davenport's *Annual Review of Political Science* survey named the "Punishment
Puzzle": dissent reliably provokes repression (the "Law of Coercive
Responsiveness"), but what repression does to dissent has been found to be
negative, positive, inverted-U, sign-switching, and nonexistent — essentially
every possible result is in print. Davenport, Sullivan and Loyle later attacked
the puzzle directly and argued the effect is contingent on temporal sequence:
using Northern Ireland events data 1968–74, repression raised dissent when
dissent had been falling, and suppressed it when dissent had been rising. That
is a resolution for one case, not for the literature.

The puzzle matters here because repression is the mechanism through which
autocratic discontent is supposed to convert into either transformation or
entrenchment. If nobody knows the sign of the repression→dissent effect, then
nobody has a defensible prior about the *direction* of the discontent→regime
effect in autocracies. Add the measurement layer: regimes strategically conceal
repression, so studies must identify effects in the presence of unobserved
treatment.

| Position | Representative claims catalogued by Davenport 2007 | Evidence base | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Repression suppresses dissent | Hibbs 1973 | Cross-national event data | Contested |
| Repression provokes dissent (backfire) | Francisco 1996; Lichbach & Gurr 1981; Ziegenhagen 1986 | Event data, various | Contested |
| Inverted-U | Muller 1985 *ASR* | Cross-national, inequality→violence peaking at intermediate repressiveness | Contested |
| Sign varies | Gupta & Venieris 1981; Moore 1998; Rasler 1996 | Time-series | Contested |
| No effect | Gurr & Moore 1997 | Cross-national | Contested |
| Effect is sequence-contingent | Davenport, Sullivan & Loyle 2012, *International Interactions* 38(4) | Northern Ireland 1968–74 events | Single-case resolution |
| Situational vs. institutional repression differ | Koopmans (as summarized in secondary sources) — UNVERIFIED exact cite | Western European protest data | Partial |

**What this paper can say about it:** finding (b) is the macro-level echo of the
punishment puzzle. Discontent reliably opens a transformation episode in an
autocracy but does not determine where it goes — exactly what one should expect
if the state's coercive response has no stable sign. The paper can frame (b) not
as a null result but as the aggregate consequence of a fifty-year-old
unresolved micro-level indeterminacy, which is a far stronger framing.

## 3. The Revolutionary-Surprise Puzzle (Kuran) — measured support in autocracies may be endogenous to stability

### State of the dispute — **THREAT**

Kuran's preference-falsification argument holds that under repression, declared
public preferences diverge from private ones, that this confers "an aura of
stability on structures vulnerable to sudden collapse," and that revolutionary
surprises are therefore structurally inevitable. Goodwin's post-Arab-Spring
assessment credits Kuran with predicting the field's collective failure to
foresee 2011. But Goodwin adds an important qualification that cuts *for* the
paper: Kuran is not claiming we cannot know whether a population dislikes a
regime — it was no mystery that Ben Ali, Mubarak and Qadhafi were widely
reviled. What is unknowable is the distribution of individual revolutionary
*thresholds*.

That distinction maps almost exactly onto findings (a) and (b). Aggregate
discontent is measurable and predicts that something will give (onset);
thresholds are not measurable and govern what happens next (direction,
outcome). This is the single most elegant theoretical framing available for the
paper's central asymmetry.

The threat lies elsewhere. If survey-measured discontent in autocracies is
systematically attenuated by falsification, and if attenuation is *worse in more
repressive and more stable autocracies*, then measured discontent is
mechanically correlated with regime weakness and the paper's autocracy
coefficient is partly a measurement artifact. There is an active measurement
literature here — list experiments, purge-based designs, and a *PSRM* article
inferring falsification from nonresponse rates — and a revisionist strand
arguing falsification is more limited than assumed and that conventional
measures sometimes outperform list experiments. This is unresolved, and it is a
live reviewer objection.

| Study | Claim | Evidence base | Bearing on this paper |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kuran 1991 *World Politics*; 1995 *Private Truths, Public Lies* (Harvard UP) | Falsification hides opposition; collapse is unanticipated | Theory + 1989 cases | Explains why onset ≠ direction |
| Kuran 1995, "Inevitability of Future Revolutionary Surprises" | No theory of revolution has predictive success | Theory | Sets a ceiling on what (b) could ever show |
| Goodwin 2011, *Swiss Political Science Review* | Kuran vindicated by Arab Spring; but aggregate dissatisfaction WAS known | Case assessment | Licenses the onset/threshold split |
| Shamaileh 2019, *International Interactions* 45 | Falsification varies with openness and with perceived revolution probability | Arab survey data, Lebanon/Saudi/Sudan comparisons | Falsification is itself endogenous to expected instability — the threat |
| Revisionist measurement work (list experiments vs. direct questions) | Falsification may be smaller than assumed | Experimental | Partially defuses the threat — UNVERIFIED as a settled finding |

**What this paper can say about it:** Kuran gives the paper its best theoretical
frame for (a)+(b). But the paper must pre-empt the endogeneity objection
explicitly: does the PPD measure behave differently in high-repression
autocracies, and would attenuation bias the autocracy coefficient up or down?
Attenuation from falsification is classically *toward* zero, which makes finding
(a) conservative — that argument should be made in the text, not left implicit.

## 4. Mass-driven vs. elite-driven backsliding — a direct challenge to H1

### State of the dispute — **THREAT**

The dominant contemporary account of autocratization is elite-led and
legalistic. Lührmann and Lindberg report that roughly 68% of contemporary
autocratization episodes beginning in democracies are led by incumbents who came
to power legally, typically through elections, and that gradual erosion under a
legal façade accounts for about 70% of third-wave episodes. Bermeo's parallel
framework replaces coups with "executive aggrandizement" and explicitly reorients
the field toward "choices and choosers" — that is, toward incumbent elites and
away from mass publics and structural conditions. Wuttke, Gavras and Schoen then
remove the mass-demand leg entirely for Europe: across 18 consolidated
democracies 1981–2018, with pre-registered analyses disentangling age, period
and cohort, support for democratic government is stable and institutional
confidence has recently grown.

Put together, these three yield a clean rival hypothesis: contemporary regime
change is supply-side. Mass discontent is neither necessary nor sufficient; what
varies is elite opportunity and willingness. That is a frontal challenge to H1.

Three things blunt it, and the paper should say all three.

First, scope. The elite-led thesis is about *autocratization in democracies*.
The paper's positive result is about *episode onset in autocracies*. The two
claims are not in contradiction; finding (a) is precisely what one would predict
if the elite-led account is right about democracies. Finding (a) and the
elite-led literature agree that mass discontent does not drive democratic
backsliding.

Second, the mechanism the paper's theory section already specifies — elites
reading public sentiment as a signal about the cost of defection (Svolik;
O'Donnell & Schmitter; Casper & Tyson) — makes mass and elite accounts
complementary rather than rival. Discontent does not need citizens in the street
to matter; it needs ministers and officers who can read a poll.

Third, Wuttke et al. themselves hedge: they find "democrats in name only" among
the young in some countries, and changed meanings of democracy — a permissive
condition for elite-led erosion, not a refutation of mass-level vulnerability.

| Position | Who | Evidence base | Sample / era | Adjudicated? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Autocratization is elite-led, legalistic, gradual | Lührmann & Lindberg 2019, *Democratization* 26(7) | V-Dem autocratization episodes 1900–2017; descriptive wave analysis | Global, third wave | No |
| Backsliding = executive aggrandizement, not mass revolt | Bermeo 2016, *Journal of Democracy* 27(1) | Conceptual/typological, post-Cold War cases | Global | Criticized for imprecision & lack of measurement strategy (Mechkova, Lührmann & Lindberg 2017 — UNVERIFIED exact cite) |
| Mass democratic support is stable, so it cannot be driving decline | Wuttke, Gavras & Schoen 2022, *BJPS* 52(1) | EVS/WVS, 18 consolidated democracies 1981–2018, pre-registered, APC decomposition | Western Europe, democracies | No — but scope is democracies only |
| Pro-*autocratic* mass mobilization does drive regime change | Hellmeier & Bernhard 2023, *CPS* 56(12) | New V-Dem expert measures of pro-democratic and pro-autocratic mobilization, 179 polities 1900–2021 | Global, long run | Reopens the mass channel from the other side |
| Mass discontent raises onset hazard in autocracies | **This paper** | PPD, ERT episodes, 136 countries 1968–2023, MOC | Global, both regime types | — |

**What this paper can say about it:** the honest framing is a division of labor,
not a victory. The paper's finding (a) *concedes* the democracies half of the
elite-led thesis and contests only the autocracies half — a half the elite-led
literature never claimed. Hellmeier & Bernhard is the paper's ally here: mass
attitudes and mass action are back in the causal story, just not as pro-democratic
forces. The risk to manage is a reviewer reading H1 as a general mass-driven
claim; the results only support a regime-conditional one, and the theory section
should be trimmed to match.

## 5. Does popular pressure democratize or entrench? — the field's largest live disagreement about direction

### State of the dispute

One literature says popular pressure liberalizes. Chenoweth and Stephan report
that across 1900–2019 roughly 51% of nonviolent revolutionary campaigns succeeded
outright versus about 26% of violent ones; Teorell finds peaceful mobilization
predicts democratic transition; Celestino and Gleditsch find nonviolent campaigns
raise transition likelihood in autocracies; Brancati links protest size to
democratization; Kadivar finds longer pre-transition mobilization produces
longer-lived democracies. This is the strongest, most replicated body of
behavioral evidence in the field.

A second literature says popular pressure entrenches. Ulfelder's event-history
models already showed in 2005 that the effect of contentious collective action
on authoritarian breakdown varies in *strength and direction* across types of
autocracy and forms of collective action — a direction-instability result that
predates and anticipates the paper's finding (b). Hellmeier and Weidmann show
autocrats deploy pro-government rallies strategically in response to opposition
mobilization; Hellmeier and Bernhard's new V-Dem measures show pro-autocratic
mobilization is itself a driver of regime transformation. Chin, Song and Wright
show that where security forces are personalized, protest is both rarer and more
likely to be repressed, and uprisings that do occur are less likely to produce
democratic transitions. Timoneda, Escribà-Folch and Chin show that failed coups
trigger rapid personalization rather than liberalization — but note that their
challenge is **elite-level, not popular**: the IV is failed coup attempts
(Powell–Thyne and Colpus data) and the DV is a latent 0–1 personalism score
(Wright 2021; Geddes, Wright & Frantz 2018). Mass mobilization appears nowhere
in their models. It belongs in this section only as an analogy for how a
survived challenge concentrates rather than disperses power, and must not be
cited as evidence about popular pressure.

And the era matters. Chenoweth's 2020 *Journal of Democracy* essay reports that
nonviolent revolutionary success rates held above 40% from the 1960s through
about 2010, peaking near 65% in the 1990s, then fell below 34% after 2010 (with
violent campaigns at 8%). If the democratizing effect of popular pressure was
real but is decaying, then the pooled cross-national estimates that support the
optimistic camp are era-averaged over a regime that no longer holds. Nobody has
adjudicated whether the "popular pressure democratizes" finding survives
restriction to the post-2010 period.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Democratizing | Chenoweth & Stephan 2011 (Columbia UP) | Nonviolent vs. violent campaign (NAVCO) | Campaign success, subsequent democracy | 323 campaigns, 1900–2006 | Nonviolent ~2× success |
| Democratizing | Teorell 2010 (Cambridge UP) | Peaceful mobilization | Democratic transition | 165 countries, 1972–2006 | Positive |
| Democratizing | Celestino & Gleditsch 2013, *JPR* 50(3) | Nonviolent campaign | Transition / autocratic breakdown | Autocracies, cross-national | Positive |
| Democratizing | Brancati 2016 (Cambridge UP) | Democracy-protest size | Democratization | 180 states, 1989–2011 | Positive |
| Democratizing | Kadivar 2018, *ASR* 83(2) | Duration of unarmed mobilization | Survival of new democracies | 112 young democracies | Positive for durability |
| **Direction unstable** | Ulfelder 2005, *IPSR* 26(3) | Contentious collective action events | Authoritarian breakdown | Global autocracies, event history | **Effect varies in sign and size by autocracy type and action type** |
| Entrenching | Hellmeier & Weidmann 2020, *CPS* 53(1) | Opposition mobilization | Pro-government rallies (MMAD) | Autocracies, cross-national TSCS | Regimes counter-mobilize |
| Entrenching | Hellmeier & Bernhard 2023, *CPS* 56(12) | Pro-autocratic mass mobilization (new V-Dem measures) | Regime transformation, democracy level | 179 polities, 1900–2021 | Pro-autocratic mobilization drives autocratization |
| Entrenching | Chin, Song & Wright 2023, *BJPS* 53(1) | Security-force personalization | Protest onset, repression, transition success | Dictatorships, cross-national | Personalized forces suppress protest and block transitions |
| Entrenching (**analogy only — elite challenge, not popular pressure**) | Timoneda, Escribà-Folch & Chin 2023, *BJPS* 53(3):878–901 | Failed coup attempts (Powell–Thyne; Colpus) | Latent personalism, 0–1 (Wright 2021; Geddes, Wright & Frantz 2018 IRT) | Dictatorships since 1946; incl. synthetic control | Failed elite challenge → rapid power concentration. **No mass-mobilization IV** |
| Era decay | Chenoweth 2020, *JoD* 31(3):69–84 | Time period | Nonviolent campaign success | 1900–2019, decade breakdown | >40% (1960s–2010), ~65% (1990s), <34% (post-2010) |

**Not adjudicated.** No study in this set jointly models the democratizing and
entrenching channels on a common event-level DV with a common sample. The camps
differ on IV (campaign vs. protest event vs. expert-coded mobilization vs.
personalization), on DV (campaign success vs. transition vs. democracy level vs.
personalism score), and on era (the optimistic results are weighted toward
1980–2010).

**What this paper can say about it — the single best fit in the file.** Finding
(b) is a direct, uncertainty-aware, event-level answer: mass discontent predicts
that an autocracy enters transformation but not whether the transformation runs
up or down the scale. That is exactly the pattern one would observe if both
camps are describing real, roughly offsetting mechanisms operating on the same
episodes. The paper is also the first to deliver this at the *attitudinal*
level, where the entrenching camp has no measure at all. The comparison to
Ulfelder 2005 is worth making explicitly: he found direction-instability with
behavioral IVs twenty years ago, and it has never been resolved.

## 6. Critical citizens: healthy skepticism or corrosion? — and a live experimental contradiction

### State of the dispute

Norris's "critical citizens"/"democratic deficit" line holds that distrust
reflects rising democratic aspirations and produces vigilant, demanding
citizens rather than regime threats. Three recent strands push back.

*Conceptual.* Bertsou argues the late-1990s answers are no longer adequate and
that *distrust* is not merely low trust — trust and distrust are distinct but
related concepts with different behavioral consequences, so the benign reading
of "low trust" cannot be extended to distrust proper.

*Macro-empirical.* Dawson and Krakoff re-examine the thesis on a cross-national
panel of 82 countries 1990–2020 with multilevel models. They find a strong
negative democracy–trust relationship that is not an artifact of
misspecification or response bias, and they reject the "containment" claim
directly: disaggregating trust into partisan and non-partisan institutions, they
find no significant difference in democracy's effect between them. Their
conclusion is that low trust in democracies may be a more acute problem than the
literature assumes.

*Experimental — and here the literature contradicts itself head-on.* Saikkonen
and Christensen ran a conjoint on prospective prime ministers in Finland
(n≈1,030), a high-satisfaction consensual democracy, and found *satisfied*
respondents less willing to tolerate democratic infringements — i.e., the
dissatisfied were the permissive group, against the critical-citizens
hypothesis. Kaftan and Gessler ran a survey experiment in Germany and Poland and
found the opposite in Poland: dissatisfied Polish citizens were more likely to
punish undemocratic behavior, while in Germany satisfaction barely correlated
with punishment at all. Both sets of authors converge on the same
reconciliation: satisfaction means something different where backsliding is
actually underway, and satisfaction-with-democracy items may lack measurement
equivalence across countries.

That reconciliation is a *context-conditional* theory of discontent's effect —
which is structurally the same claim this paper makes at the macro level.

| Study | Position | Design | Sample | Finding |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Norris 2011 (Cambridge UP) | Critical citizens are benign | Comparative survey | 50+ societies | Deficit → engagement, not breakdown |
| Bertsou 2019, *EPSR* | Distrust ≠ low trust; benign reading unwarranted | Conceptual + qualitative (*Societies* 9(4):72 companion) | 3 national contexts | Technical / ethical / interest-based evaluations |
| Dawson & Krakoff 2024, *Democratization* 31(1):90–112 | Distrust in democracies is not contained | Multilevel TSCS | 82 countries, 1990–2020 | Democracy → lower trust; no partisan/non-partisan containment |
| Saikkonen & Christensen 2023, *PRQ* | Dissatisfied are MORE permissive | Conjoint, n≈1,030 | Finland (no backsliding) | Contradicts critical-citizens thesis |
| Kaftan & Gessler 2024/25, *Government and Opposition* 60(2):358–381 | Dissatisfied are MORE protective | Survey experiment | Germany & Poland (Poland backsliding) | Supports critical-citizens thesis in Poland; null in Germany |
| **This paper** | Effect of discontent is regime-conditional | Discrete-time hazard, MOC | 136 countries, 1968–2023 | Destabilizing in autocracies only |

**Nobody has tested this at the regime-event level.** Every study above stops at
attitudes, vignettes, or hypothetical vote choice. None regresses coded regime
events on measured discontent. Note also a citation correction for the companion
lit review: Saikkonen & Christensen 2023 is in *Political Research Quarterly*,
not *Comparative Political Studies*.

**What this paper can say about it:** it is the first regime-event test, and its
answer (a)+(e) is the macro analogue of the Finland/Poland reconciliation. Where
citizens have exit through elections, discontent is inert with respect to regime
survival; where they do not, it is not. The paper can further note that (e) —
support for democracy protects democracies while discontent does not endanger
them — is a *sharper* version of the critical-citizens thesis than Norris
offered, because it separates the two attitudes empirically rather than
conflating them.

## 7. The support-and-democracy replication war — unresolved, and the paper is a combatant

### State of the dispute

This is the most consequential open methodological fight for the paper, because
its own finding (d) is an instance of it.

Claassen 2020 (*AJPS*) reported that latent democratic support raises subsequent
democracy levels; Claassen 2020 (*APSR*) reported a thermostatic effect in the
reverse direction. Tai, Hu and Solt re-estimated both while propagating
measurement uncertainty from the latent-variable model into the regressions
using the method of composition, and found no support for either conclusion —
and that adding survey data to shrink the uncertainty does not restore
significance. Their replication was "primary": they re-collected all source data
because the Dataverse files held only point estimates, and they report
correcting miscodings along the way. Hu, Tai and Solt (*PSRM*) separately showed
Claassen's thermostatic result does not survive alternative but defensible
choices about ordinal-response coding and non-respondent treatment — researcher
degrees of freedom rather than measurement uncertainty.

Claassen's counter-moves are threefold. He issued an *AJPS* erratum in 2024
correcting data errors and reported that the corrected estimates barely moved
(model 1.1: 0.267→0.273, SE 0.094) and confirm the original conclusions. He
posted a correction to the *APSR* piece's underlying data after Hu, Tai and Solt
identified errors. And in a working paper on including measurement uncertainty
in TSCS analyses he argues that MOC is not the best available method — a
"unified model" estimating latent variables and structural parameters jointly is
— while asserting that Tai, Hu and Solt's own expanded dataset contains errors
and inconsistencies requiring re-collection. Meanwhile Tai, Hu and Solt's *APSR*
letter itself received a corrigendum, whose revised results reaffirm the null.

So: two teams, mutually accusing each other of data errors, disagreeing about
the correct uncertainty-propagation method, with erratum and corrigendum on both
sides and no third-party adjudication. Hu, Tai, Ko, Woo and Solt then escalated
to the measurement model itself, arguing in *Research & Politics* that a
one-dimensional latent variable does not capture democratic support, using 41
items from 221 survey datasets across 136 countries.

| Item | Claim | Method | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Claassen 2020, *AJPS* 64(1):118–134 | Support → democratic survival | Latent IRT support + dynamic panel, ~135 countries | Erratum 2024 (*AJPS*, doi 10.1111/ajps.12828); author says conclusions hold |
| Claassen 2020, *APSR* 114(1) | Democracy → support (thermostatic) | TSCS/ECM | Data corrected after Hu/Tai/Solt |
| Tai, Hu & Solt 2024, *APSR* 118(1):512–518 | Both results vanish with measurement uncertainty | Method of composition, re-collected data | Corrigendum reaffirms null |
| Hu, Tai & Solt 2024, *PSRM* | Thermostatic result is researcher-degrees-of-freedom | Multiverse over coding choices | Unrebutted in the verified set |
| Claassen (working paper, 2022) | MOC is second-best; unified model preferable; THS data flawed | Methodological comparison | **Not published in the verified set — UNVERIFIED status** |
| Hu, Tai, Ko, Woo & Solt 2025, *Research & Politics* | One dimension is the wrong measurement model | 2-PL ordinal IRT, 41 items, 221 datasets, 136 countries | New front |

**What this paper can say about it:** finding (d) is a live demonstration of the
stakes. Two substantively important results — discontent → autocratization-episode
onset, and discontent → autocratic deepening — are significant naively and null
once uncertainty is propagated. This is the paper's strongest methodological
contribution and should be foregrounded, not buried: it shows the Tai–Hu–Solt
critique is not a one-off about Claassen's data but a general property of
latent-opinion-as-regressor designs. Two cautions. First, the paper's own author
list overlaps the Tai–Hu–Solt side; the framing must be about the method, not
the dispute. Second, if Claassen's "unified model" position is right that MOC is
conservative relative to a joint estimator, a reviewer can argue that findings
(b) and (d) are *under*powered rather than genuinely null. The paper should
address that head-on — ideally by reporting whether the naive-vs-MOC gap is
driven by the width of the PPD posterior in specific regime-years.

## 8. Does the regime coding change the answer? — an old warning, now with an event-level demonstration

### State of the dispute

The measurement literature has warned for two decades that democracy indices are
correlated but not interchangeable. Casper and Tufis tested one democratization
model across Vanhanen, Polity IV, and Freedom House in 120+ countries 1951–1992
and found that most results were not robust to the choice of index, concluding
that measure choice partly explains why democracy debates never resolve. Cheibub,
Gandhi and Vreeland opened *Democracy and Dictatorship Revisited* by explicitly
disagreeing with the interchangeability view and demonstrated the point by
replicating published studies under alternative regime measures. Boese compared
Polity IV, Freedom House and V-Dem on conceptualization, measurement scale and
aggregation, finding high agreement on overlapping observations but substantial
divergence in coverage. Vaccaro extended the tests to four contemporary measures
and found the sharpest version of the warning: convergence is high, but
interchangeability is *weak and has been declining since the 1980s*; he
recommends validating findings across multiple measures whenever theory cannot
select one.

The dispute then escalated from indices to the backsliding narrative itself.
Little and Meng argue in *PS* that objective indicators — notably incumbent
performance in elections — show little evidence of global democratic decline
over the past decade, and float time-varying coder bias as the explanation for
expert-coded declines. Knutsen, Marquardt, Seim and the V-Dem team reply in the
same issue that time-varying expert bias is highly unlikely to drive the
observed declines, that no measure of democracy is free of human judgment, and
that L&M's indicators are ill-suited as a benchmark. Miller adds that because
recent decline is concentrated *within* democracies, indicators that only
separate democracies from autocracies are blind to it by construction. The
exchange is unresolved and has drawn commentary from philosophy of science.

| Study | Claim | Evidence base | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Casper & Tufis 2003, *Political Analysis* 11(2):196–203 | Highly correlated indices give different results | One democratization model, 3 indices, 120+ countries, 1951–92 | Most findings not robust |
| Cheibub, Gandhi & Vreeland 2010, *Public Choice* 143:67–101 | Measures are not interchangeable; DD introduced | Replications of published studies under alternative codings | Choice of measure matters |
| Boese 2019, *IASR* 22(2):95–127 | V-Dem outperforms Polity2/FH on concept, scale, aggregation | Methodological comparison | High agreement, divergent coverage |
| Vaccaro 2021, *European Political Science* 20:666–684 | Convergence high, **interchangeability weak and falling since the 1980s** | 4 measures, statistical properties + over-time convergence | Validate across measures |
| Little & Meng 2024, *PS* 57(2):149–161 | Objective indicators show no global backsliding; coder bias suspected | Incumbent election performance and other objective indicators | Contested |
| Knutsen et al. 2024, *PS* 57(2):162–177 (V-Dem WP 140) | Expert-bias explanation implausible; no measure is judgment-free | Theory + empirical tests of V-Dem coder behavior | Contested |
| Miller (2024), *PS* — "How Little and Meng's Objective Approach Fails in Democracies" | Objective indicators cannot detect within-democracy decline | Conceptual + empirical | Contested |
| **This paper, Table 4** | ERT, DD and BMR identify largely disjoint sets of completed transitions | Event-level overlap of coded regime changes | Original evidence |

**Note the citation correction:** Little & Meng 2024 is in *PS: Political
Science & Politics*, not the *APSR*. The task brief and any draft text carrying
"APSR" should be fixed.

**What this paper can say about it — and where it is exposed.** The paper's
event-overlap table is a genuine contribution to a twenty-year-old
methodological argument that has been conducted almost entirely at the level of
correlations and index properties rather than at the level of *events*. Reporting
that only 11% of ERT democratization-ending episodes contain a DD or BMR
democratization event, that only 20–28% of DD/BMR democratizations fall inside a
corresponding ERT episode, and that DD and BMR disagree substantially with each
other, is the sharpest concrete illustration of Vaccaro's "weak
interchangeability" claim in the regime-change literature. It should be framed
as such.

**THREAT.** Finding (c) is the paper's most exposed point. Reporting that
discontent predicts autocracy-to-democracy transitions under DD and BMR while
predicting neither direction nor outcome under ERT invites the reading that the
substantive conclusion is an artifact of DV choice. The event-overlap table
defuses this only if the paper commits to a principled reason for privileging
ERT — and the Data section currently justifies ERT on conceptual grounds
(sustained episodes vs. annual snapshots) without pre-registering that
commitment against the alternatives. Vaccaro's own recommendation is validation
across measures, not selection of one; the paper does the validation and then
picks a winner. A reviewer will notice. The strongest available defense is that
DD and BMR measure a *narrower* event (completed transition across a dichotomous
threshold) that is a subset-in-principle of the ERT outcome space, and that
finding (c) is therefore consistent with (b) if discontent affects the
probability of crossing a threshold conditional on an episode already being
underway — a claim the paper could actually test by conditioning DD/BMR
transitions on ERT episode status.

## 9. Asserted but never properly tested

Claims that are canonical in the discontent literature and for which no
large-N, event-level test appears in the verified record. For each I state the
missing test concretely.

| # | Canonical claim | Where asserted | What is missing |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Legitimacy is a precondition of regime survival; its erosion produces breakdown | Lipset 1959; Easton 1965/1975; repeated throughout the trust literature | A hazard model of coded regime events on *measured* legitimacy/diffuse support with standard controls. Lipset's empirics are development–democracy correlations; Easton is conceptual. **This paper supplies it.** |
| 2 | Declining democratic support is an "early warning sign" of breakdown | Foa & Mounk 2016, 2017 *JoD*; and their 2018 CES paper establishing that support levels move | Nobody has modeled breakdown as the outcome. Foa & Mounk's own inference runs from survey trends plus Venezuela as an illustrative counterfactual. The missing test is whether the **trend/slope** of support, not its level, predicts autocratization onset. *This paper tests levels, so the gap remains open — and is an obvious extension.* |
| 3 | "Democracies without democrats are not sustainable" | Stated as the opening premise of Wuttke, Gavras & Schoen 2022 *BJPS* | Never estimated as a survival model. The premise licenses the whole deconsolidation literature and has no event-level test. Finding (e) is the closest existing evidence and it is *supportive*. |
| 4 | Polarization erodes democracy by disabling the electoral check | Svolik 2019 *JoD*; Graham & Svolik 2020 *APSR*; Krishnarajan 2023 *APSR* | All three are individual-level (conjoints, natural experiment, survey experiments). No large-N test of measured polarization → coded autocratization onset appears in the verified record. V-Dem's `v2cacamps` makes it feasible. UNVERIFIED whether such a test exists in 2025–26 work. |
| 5 | Repression provokes the backlash it is meant to prevent | Asserted in the paper's own Theory section via Davenport 2007 | Davenport's point is the opposite — the sign is *unknown* (§2). No cross-national event-level test of repression at t → regime-transformation onset at t+1. The paper should soften this sentence. |
| 6 | Measured opinion in autocracies is uninformative because of preference falsification | Kuran 1991/1995; standard reviewer objection | The claim has never been tested by showing that measured support *fails* to predict regime events in autocracies. Finding (a) is arguably the first large-N evidence *against* strong falsification pessimism, since a badly falsified measure should not predict onset at all. |
| 7 | Popular mobilization democratizes | Teorell 2010; Chenoweth & Stephan 2011; Brancati 2016; Celestino & Gleditsch 2013 | Chenoweth 2020 documents success rates collapsing from >40% (1960s–2010) and ~65% (1990s) to <34% post-2010. Nobody has re-estimated the democratization models on the post-2010 sample. The optimistic consensus rests on an era that may have ended. |
| 8 | The outcome of a transition moment is contingent on elite cohesion, opposition organization, and coercive-apparatus loyalty, not mass sentiment | O'Donnell & Schmitter 1986; Przeworski 1991; Treisman 2020 | Asserted as a scope condition for forty years, never tested as a *differential* prediction — i.e., the same IV predicting onset but not direction. **This paper's finding (b) is that test.** |
| 9 | Political trust is falling and this endangers democracy | The trust literature generally | van Ham, Thomassen, Aarts & Andeweg 2017 (OUP) find the evidence for secular decline "limited, questionable, or absent." So the antecedent is contested *and* the consequent is untested. |
