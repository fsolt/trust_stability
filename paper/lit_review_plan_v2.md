# Literature Review — revision plan v2

Supersedes `lit_review_plan.md`, whose spine was methodological (the field tests the wrong
dependent variable). This one is substantive and follows the four-part structure requested:
what the literature has found → what puzzle remains → why solving it matters → why a
large-scope time-series cross-sectional design is the instrument that can settle it.

**Spine, one sentence:** *That lost legitimacy destabilizes regimes is the founding intuition
of the political-support literature, it has never been tested against the outcome it
predicts, and the place the theory says it should bite hardest is the place the field has
deliberately stopped looking.*

**Target length ~1,200 words**, down from the current draft's 1,814. Four sections plus a
three-sentence coda. Roughly one paragraph per section, two at most.

---

## Three drafting rules for this revision

**1. Group the citations; do not walk through studies one at a time.** The current draft
gives most works their own sentence with a design description and a finding. Replace that
with bundled claims: one sentence states what a body of work has established, the citation
bundle carries the evidence. Reserve an individual sentence for a work only where it does
something no other work does. At most four studies should get their own sentence in the
whole section, and Booth & Seligson must be one of them.

**2. No counts anywhere in the prose.** Cut every survey total, country total, year span,
event count, and interval. The arguments below are all available qualitatively, and §4 in
particular reads better as a chain of necessities than as a table of magnitudes. Where a
number was doing real work, replace it with its consequence: not "ninety onsets across
fifty-six years" but "regime change is rare enough that only a long, broad panel yields
enough cases to model."

**3. Terminology.** Do not use "autocracy," "autocratic," "authoritarian," or "dictatorship."
Two distinct substitutions, because two distinct things are being named:

- **The literature's scope restriction** → "Western democracies," "advanced industrial
  democracies," "established democracies." This is the more accurate phrasing anyway; it is
  what the field's own titles say.
- **This paper's regime-type split** → "non-democracies" and "democracies." This cannot
  become "non-Western democracies," which names a different set: the analysis classifies
  regimes by whether power is contested through elections, so India and Brazil sit on the
  democracy side of the split while China and Saudi Arabia sit on the other. Using
  "non-Western democracies" for the second cell would misdescribe the design and the finding.
  If "non-democracies" reads badly, the workable alternatives are "closed regimes" or
  "regimes without electoral alternation." Pick one and use it everywhere, including the
  Theory, Data, and Results sections, so the manuscript carries one vocabulary throughout.

## §1. What is known (~300 words, one paragraph)

Three bundled claims, in an order that crescendos from solid to absent.

**Mass behavior moves regimes, and it moves them toward democracy.** The most replicated
result in the field, and the citation bundle carries it: Teorell; Chenoweth & Stephan;
Celestino & Gleditsch; Brancati; Kadivar. One clause is enough to note that Kadivar runs the
other way from a destabilization story, since mobilization makes new democracies *more*
durable.

**Mass attitudes move democracy scores, but the finding is fragile.** Bundle Welzel,
Inglehart & Welzel, Qi & Shin, and Claassen. Then one sentence for Tai, Hu & Solt, which
earns it: propagating measurement uncertainty removes the result, and more survey data does
not bring it back.

**Mass attitudes have never entered a model of a coded regime event.** Bundle the canonical
regime-survival literature (Przeworski & Limongi; Boix & Stokes; Gasiorowski; Svolik; Houle;
Geddes et al.; Gassebner et al.) in a single citation and state the absence once.

**Close the paragraph on Belkin & Schofer**, which is the one place this is visible rather
than merely assertable. Their coup-risk model does carry a legitimacy term, and they build it
from the competitiveness and regulation of the political system, with the age of the regime
as an alternative. When this literature reaches for legitimacy, it measures institutional
structure rather than asking any citizen what they believe. That sentence shows the gap
instead of claiming it, and it hands §2 its opening. *(Verified against the article.)*

## §2. The puzzle (~300 words, one paragraph)

Two tensions, both unresolved, and unresolved for the same reason.

**The legitimacy paradox.** Lipset and Easton make legitimacy a precondition of stability.
The claim was asserted rather than tested: Lipset's own evidence is development-democracy
correlation. When Booth & Seligson went looking, disaffected citizens neither withdrew from
politics nor turned to destabilizing protest. Norris finds the democratic deficit produces
critical engagement rather than breakdown. And the premise itself is contested, since the
secular decline in support may not be happening. Neither link in the canonical chain is
established.

**The direction indeterminacy.** Where popular pressure does move regimes, the literature
cannot agree which way. Bundle the dissenting camp — Ulfelder, Hellmeier & Bernhard, Chin,
Song & Wright — against the optimistic bundle from §1, and let Davenport's punishment puzzle
carry the underlying point in one sentence: nobody knows the sign of the repression-to-dissent
effect, so nobody has a defensible prior about direction.

**Close by naming what unites them.** Booth & Seligson looked at a handful of countries in a
single year, at the level of individual behavior. The behavioral camps disagree partly because
they use different samples, outcomes, and eras. Both tensions are open because the tests have
been small and aimed at the wrong cases.

⚠️ Describe Booth & Seligson precisely: a small, single-year, individual-level design. It is a
null that was *designed to find* the effect, which is what gives it force, and it is not a
well-powered macro test. Do not let it carry more weight than its design supports.

## §3. Why settling it matters (~300 words, one paragraph)

The section the current draft lacks entirely, and what turns the paper from gap-filling into
adjudication. Three stakes, and keep each to two or three sentences.

**It decides between two theories of why regimes survive.** The legitimacy account says
regimes endure because citizens grant them rightfulness. The rival says they endure because
institutions make compliance rational, as in Przeworski's account of losers' consent under
institutional uncertainty. These make a *differential* prediction: if compliance rather than
legitimacy does the work, discontent should be inert precisely where institutions supply an
alternative government, and consequential where they do not. The contrast is testable and
has not been tested.

**It is the first test of a long-standing scope condition.** O'Donnell & Schmitter,
Przeworski, and Treisman have all held that the outcome of a transition moment turns on elite
cohesion, opposition organization, and the loyalty of the coercive apparatus rather than on
mass sentiment. Asserted for four decades, never tested as what it is, a prediction that one
variable should forecast onset without forecasting direction. **Calibrate — see the warning.**

**The theory's strongest prediction sits in its least-examined cases, and the field says so
itself.** Discontent should matter most where citizens cannot vote their way out. Yet the
most comprehensive global trust series confines its trend analysis to countries democratic in
most years, and scholars of regime legitimacy state in print that they study what regimes
*claim* because citizens' beliefs are hard to recover from surveys there. The restriction is
a documented retreat with a stated reason, not an oversight. This is where the current
draft's opening paragraph on the critical-citizens literature belongs.

## §4. Why this requires a large, long panel (~300 words, one paragraph)

Make scale a necessity argument, as a chain rather than a list. No counts.

Regime change is rare, so only a broad and long panel yields enough cases to model at all.
Booth & Seligson's design could not carry the macro question, and reviewers noted they had to
enter country-level variables one at a time. The regime-type contrast then needs both kinds of
case in a single model, since the claim is comparative and neither a single country nor a
single region can supply it. Separating whether a transformation begins from where it goes
divides the cases again, and the outcome distinctions divide them further still, which is why
the estimates need a penalized estimator rather than a conventional one. Long coverage is
required for a second reason: discontent is defined by its durability and regime
transformation unfolds over years, so short windows cannot see either.

Then the measurement material, demoted from the current draft's second slot to **three
sentences here**. Pooling many survey series is what makes coverage outside the Western
democracies possible at all; doing so introduces measurement uncertainty that has to be
carried into the estimates rather than ignored; and because every documented bias in these
settings runs toward overstating support, the resulting estimate is conservative. Add one
clause conceding that the threat is bias that moves *with* the outcome, not bias as such.

Close on Sato & Wiebrecht: episode onset split by regime type is already a published
apparatus, so the contribution is the variable and the cases, not the machinery.

## Coda (~3 sentences)

Name what the paper does in one sentence, tied to §3's stakes rather than to a list of design
choices, and close on a sentence that sets up the Theory section's opening question without
answering it. The current draft's final two sentences already do this and can be kept nearly
as they stand.

## Calibration warnings

**1. The direction result is underpowered, and §3's second stake must be written to match.**
The intervals for the direction-specific estimates contain the main model's point estimate,
so the data cannot distinguish no directional effect from a directional effect as large as
the headline finding. The section may say the design is the first that can *pose* the
onset-versus-direction question and that the result is *consistent with* the scope condition.
It must not say the paper settles it. Write "the data do not resolve direction," never
"discontent does not push transformation in either direction."

**2. Related, and outside this section:** the Results section currently states the strong
version. That is absence of evidence written as evidence of absence, and it should be
softened.

**3. Claim the legitimacy puzzle as regime-conditional, not overturned.** Booth & Seligson's
null replicates among democracies; the classical expectation survives among the others. The
puzzle is localized rather than refuted.

**4. The elite-led backsliding literature is a division of labor, not a rival.** Its central
finding concerns erosion beginning in democracies, which is exactly where this paper also
finds nothing. Concede that half in a clause.

**5. Standing overclaim bans.** Never write that the attitude literature outside the Western
democracies is under-developed; it is large, and thin in one specific cell. Never write that
the literature ignores those cases. Always attach the dependent-variable restriction to any
statement of the gap.

## Flagged for the authors, outside this section

1. The paper never reports its estimation-sample size anywhere. A routine referee request.
2. The results are not reproducible from the repository as committed: `.rds` is gitignored, so
   the objects the qmd reads do not exist and it will fail to render at the `onset-effects`
   chunk.
3. The Data section's survey and item totals do not match this repository's input file;
   they appear to be quoted from the source paper. Verify which release is described.
4. The outcome-specific cells are very small relative to the number of predictors. Describe
   them as suggestive.
5. The GWF results cannot reject anything. The scripts already label them exploratory; the
   manuscript text should match.
6. Panel coverage is thin at both the beginning and the end of the period, so avoid claims
   about long-run or most-recent dynamics.
