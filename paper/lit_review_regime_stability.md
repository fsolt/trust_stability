## Part 1. POLITICAL DISCONTENT (IV) → regime (in)stability

Scope: IV must be discontent-type — dissatisfaction with democracy/government, political distrust, disaffection, grievance, taste for revolt — or its behavioral expression. Diffuse democratic support is Part 2.

### Family A — attitudinal discontent aggregates → regime outcomes (macro)

| Study | Discontent IV & measurement | DV & measurement | Design & sample | Key controls | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Booth & Seligson 2009, *The Legitimacy Puzzle in Latin America* (Cambridge UP) | Legitimacy in 6 dimensions incl. dissatisfaction/disaffection (LAPOP) | Democratic stability/survival (tested at individual behavioral level) | Survey, 8 Latin American nations, 2004 | Multidimensional legitimacy structure, demographics | **Null / paradox.** Low legitimacy does NOT threaten survival; the book's point is that the expected legitimacy→breakdown link is empirically absent. |
| Dalton 2004, *Democratic Challenges, Democratic Choices* (Oxford UP) | Eroding political support/trust (cross-time survey trends, OECD) | System consequences (participation, protest, governance) — NOT coded regime events | Descriptive trends, advanced democracies | Cohort/value change, expectations | **No breakdown documented.** Declining trust = "critical/dissatisfied democrats"; consequence is changed participation, not collapse. Not a hazard model. |
| Norris 2011, *Democratic Deficit* (Cambridge UP) | "Democratic deficit" = aspiration–performance gap (distrust, dissatisfaction), 50+ societies | Consequences for citizenship/governance (discussed, not event-modeled) | Comparative survey analysis | Demand/information/supply sources | **Challenges the disaffection-crisis thesis**; deficit fuels critical engagement, not documented breakdown. |
| Torcal & Montero (eds.) 2006, *Political Disaffection in Contemporary Democracies* (Routledge) | Political disaffection (institutional distrust, inefficacy, cynicism) | "Consequences for democracy" (conceptual/behavioral) | Edited comparative volume | Social vs. institutional trust separation | **Descriptive/conceptual**; disaffection stable and structural; no breakdown hazards estimated. |
| Lipset 1959, *APSR* 53(1) | Legitimacy (belief in moral title to rule), undermined by poor performance | Democratic stability | Cross-national correlational + theory | Development, effectiveness | **Theory + loose correlation.** Legitimacy×effectiveness→stability asserted; empirics are development–democracy correlations, not measured-discontent tests. |
| Przeworski 1991, *Democracy and the Market* (Cambridge UP) | (Counter-theory) losers' compliance from institutional uncertainty, NOT legitimacy | Democratic stability (self-enforcing equilibrium) | Game-theoretic/analytical | — | **Theoretical counter** to the discontent→instability canon: stability rests on strategic compliance, not diffuse legitimacy. |

### Family B — individual-level / experimental mechanism studies (erosion tolerance; NOT macro tests)

| Study | Discontent IV & measurement | DV & measurement | Design & sample | Key controls | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Graham & Svolik 2020, *APSR* 114(2) | Partisanship/polarization, policy extremism | Willingness to punish co-partisan democratic-norm violators | Candidate-choice conjoint + Montana 2017 natural experiment (US) | Partisan strength, policy distance | **Significant erosion mechanism**: only ~13% punish; punishment falls with polarization. |
| Svolik 2019, *J. of Democracy* 30(3) | Polarization (principles traded for partisan gains) | Public's capacity to check authoritarian incumbents | Formal + experimental illustrations (US/Venezuela) | — | **Mechanism**: polarization erodes the electoral check; questions "support for democracy" measures. |
| Krishnarajan 2023, *APSR* 117(2) | Policy disagreement/partisan motivation | Approval + *perception* of undemocratic behavior | Survey experiments, US + 22 democracies | Partisanship, policy prefs | **Significant**: citizens rationalize co-partisan undemocratic acts as "democratic." |
| Saikkonen & Christensen 2023, *Political Research Quarterly* 76(1):127–142 (DOI 10.1177/10659129211073592) | Political dissatisfaction, polarization, congruence | Tolerance of elite norm transgressions | Conjoint, Finland (n≈1,030) | Partisanship, congruence | **Sign consistent with discontent thesis but modest**: satisfied respondents LESS tolerant of infringements; single high-trust case. |

### Family C — behavioral discontent (protest, unrest, revolt taste) → regime change

| Study | Discontent IV & measurement | DV & measurement | Design & sample | Key controls | Result |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gurr 1970, *Why Men Rebel* (Princeton UP) | Relative deprivation (attitudinal discontent, theorized) | Political violence/civil strife | Theory + later cross-national tests | Coercion, institutions, legitimacy | **Theory; empirical record mixed-to-negative** — RD's statistical program produced mostly negative results; Gurr revised the theory. |
| Muller 1985, *ASR* 50(1) | Income inequality (structural grievance proxy) | Political violence (event measures) | Cross-national statistical | Regime repressiveness (curvilinear) | **Significant**: inequality → violence; peaks at intermediate repressiveness. Grievance structural, not attitudinal. |
| MacCulloch 2004, *AJPS* 48(4) | **Survey-measured "taste for revolt"** by income | Individual revolutionary preference | Micro-data, ~250k respondents | GDP pc, employment, demographics | **Significant**: income and GDP growth reduce revolt taste. | MacCulloch 2005, *JLE* 48(1) | Same, focus on inequality | Individual revolutionary preference | Micro-data multi-country | GDP pc, controls | **Significant**: inequality raises revolution taste. Same caveat. |
| Kadivar 2018, *ASR* 83(2) | Duration of unarmed mass mobilization (event data) | Survival of new democracies (survival analysis) | 112 young democracies, 80 countries | Regime type, development | **Significant, POSITIVE for durability**: longer mobilization → longer-lived democracy. |
| Brancati 2016, *Democracy Protests* (Cambridge UP) | Economic-crisis-driven democracy protests (events) | Accommodation, incumbent exit, democratization | 180 states, 1989–2011 | Crisis severity, elections, opposition | **Significant**: larger protests → democratization; crisis works via protest size. |
| Celestino & Gleditsch 2013, *JPR* 50(3) | Nonviolent vs. violent campaigns (behavioral) | Transition to democracy / autocratic breakdown | Cross-national, autocracies | International environment, regime traits | **Significant**: nonviolent protest raises transition likelihood. |
| Chenoweth & Stephan 2011, *Why Civil Resistance Works* (Columbia UP) | Nonviolent vs. violent campaigns (NAVCO) | Campaign success/regime change; durability | 323 campaigns, 1900–2006 | Size, regime type | **Significant**: nonviolent ~2× more successful; durable democracies follow. |
| Teorell 2010, *Determinants of Democratization* (Cambridge UP) | Peaceful popular mobilization (behavioral) | Democratic transition | 160+ countries, 1972–2006 | Development, crisis, diffusion | **Significant, positive** for democratization. |

### Patterns (Part 1)

1. **The attitudinal-aggregate → regime-event cell is effectively EMPTY.** No Family-A study regresses coded regime events on a discontent aggregate and finds destabilization. Booth & Seligson was designed to find the link and concluded it is absent (the "legitimacy puzzle"); Dalton and Norris document changed participation, not breakdown; Torcal & Montero is conceptual. The nearest macro test (Claassen, Part 2) uses diffuse support — out of scope — and is itself overturned by the measurement-uncertainty reanalysis.
2. **The canon asserts what has not been shown.** Lipset (legitimacy) and Gurr (relative deprivation) supply the discontent→instability intuition, but Lipset's empirics are development correlations and Gurr's RD program failed statistically. Przeworski 1991 is a ready-made theoretical counter: stability rests on strategic compliance, not legitimacy.
3. **Only the behavioral route delivers consistent macro effects** (Kadivar, Teorell, Celestino & Gleditsch, Chenoweth & Stephan, Brancati) — but discontent there is measured via protest events or structural proxies, not attitudes, and the direction is mostly democratizing (autocratic breakdown/transition), not democratic breakdown.
4. **Modern work retreats to the individual level** (Graham & Svolik; Krishnarajan): mechanism evidence that discontent/polarization can enable erosion, without macro regime-outcome tests.

→ Positioning implication: a comprehensive, uncertainty-aware panel test of attitudinal discontent against event-based regime DVs does not yet exist; this paper supplies it.

## Part 2. Adjacent literature: DEMOCRATIC SUPPORT (IV) → regime (in)stability

Rows are grouped: **(A)** studies that directly test aggregate/mass opinion → regime outcome; **(B)** the deconsolidation debate (opinion trends read as instability signals) and its rebuttals; **(C)** boundary cases verified and characterized (opinion is DV not IV, or opinion never enters).

| Study (authors, year, journal) | IV & its measurement | DV & its measurement | Design & sample | Key controls | Result (direction; significance; fragility in reanalyses) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **(A)** Claassen 2020, *AJPS* 64(1):118–134 — "Does Public Support Help Democracy Survive?" | Latent **democratic support** (diffuse support) estimated by a Bayesian latent-variable model pooling cross-national survey items; smooth country-year panel, 135 countries, up to 29 years | **Change in level of democracy**, V-Dem Liberal Democracy Index (0–100) | TSCS panel; dynamic models with lagged democracy; ~135 countries | Prior democracy, GDP/development, regional democracy, %Muslim, resource dependence | **Positive & significant**; stronger for *endurance* than *emergence*. **Fragile**: 2024 erratum corrected data errors (conclusions held per Claassen); Tai, Hu & Solt 2024 overturn it once measurement uncertainty is propagated. |

Boundary case: classic event-DV model, opinion absent. |

**Patterns (Part 2).** On-target positive findings concentrate in the older cultural-values tradition (FH-based *level* DVs) and Claassen 2020a — the latter overturned by Tai–Hu–Solt once measurement uncertainty is propagated. **No verified opinion-IV study uses discrete regime-event DVs (GWF, ERT, coups).** The two studies that looked hardest for a destabilizing effect of disaffection (Booth & Seligson; Norris) explicitly conclude it does not materialize. The "discontent destabilizes" intuition has essentially no robust confirmed support in this verified set.

| Ulfelder 2007, *CPS* 40(8) | Autocratic survival/breakdown; ACLP-style | Event-history by regime type; global autocracies | Resource income share, region, Muslim-majority | Resource wealth robustly reduces autocratic breakdown. |

**Patterns (Part 3).** DV operationalization moved in three waves: dichotomous ACLP/Polity survival (1990s–2000s) → GWF autocratic breakdown + event data (coups, PITF; late 2000s–2010s) → V-Dem continuous/episode DVs (ERT; 2019–). Standard controls: GDP pc, growth/recession, prior transitions, regional diffusion; later resource dependence, inequality, executive type. Gassebner et al.'s EBA shows most controls are individually fragile — only growth, income, and past transitions are robust. **Public-opinion variables: a confirmed gap — none of the 18 studies include mass opinion as a predictor.** Regime-durability models never test whether discontent predicts breakdown net of standard controls; that is the gap this paper occupies.
